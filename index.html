100 lenses

Order
  1. 01Technology as transformation

    Interpretation

    Technology does more than alter how a task is performed. It can transform behavior, institutions, environments, identities, expectations, and the meanings attached to an activity.

    Catalyst

    Beyond its intended function, what does this technology transform—and who benefits from, resists, or bears the cost of that transformation?

  2. 02Technology as introspection

    Interpretation

    Technology can operate as a mirror. What people build and how they use it disclose their desires, fears, assumptions, habits, and definitions of progress.

    Catalyst

    What does this technology reveal about its makers and users that they might prefer not to acknowledge?

  3. 03Technology as simulation

    Interpretation

    A simulation renders part of reality manageable by modelling it. Yet every model simplifies, excludes, and interprets; the danger begins when the representation is treated as the world itself.

    Catalyst

    What disappears when this simulation becomes a substitute for direct experience, situated knowledge, or material reality?

  4. 04Technology as tribulation

    Interpretation

    Technology can be experienced as a trial: a source of difficulty, instability, dependence, exclusion, or suffering. Such hardship is often dismissed as a temporary cost of progress.

    Catalyst

    Whose tribulation is being normalized as an acceptable price for someone else’s convenience or advancement?

  5. 05Technology as interpolation

    Interpretation

    Technology inserts itself between people and the world. It also fills gaps by estimating what is absent—predicting missing values, intentions, identities, or futures from incomplete evidence.

    Catalyst

    When a system fills in the blanks about a person or situation, who authorized the story it produces?

  6. 06Technology as transpiration

    Interpretation

    Technology has a metabolism. It draws in energy, water, minerals, labor, and information, then releases heat, emissions, waste, and altered environments. Its apparently clean surface conceals material circulation.

    Catalyst

    What must pass through the technological system for its interface to appear effortless, weightless, and clean?

  7. 07Technology as consternation

    Interpretation

    Technology can produce uncertainty, anxiety, and moral unease. Rather than treating consternation as resistance to innovation, it may be understood as a diagnostic response to unresolved consequences.

    Catalyst

    Which forms of discomfort should be listened to as knowledge rather than eliminated as obstacles to adoption?

  8. 08Technology as alteration

    Interpretation

    Alteration emphasizes incremental change. Technologies may quietly modify language, habits, spaces, relationships, and expectations until accumulated adjustments become a profound transformation.

    Catalyst

    Which seemingly minor alteration could become consequential once it is repeated, normalized, or made difficult to reverse?

  9. 09Technology as approximation

    Interpretation

    Many technologies act through estimates, averages, proxies, probabilities, and categories. Approximation may be necessary, but it becomes dangerous when uncertainty is concealed behind authoritative outputs.

    Catalyst

    Who is most likely to be harmed when an approximation is presented or acted upon as an exact truth?

  10. 10Technology as proclamation

    Interpretation

    Technologies do not merely report reality; they can proclaim what is true, valuable, normal, safe, relevant, or possible. Rankings, scores, alerts, and recommendations acquire institutional authority.

    Catalyst

    Who gave this system the authority to make declarations, and what forms of knowledge cannot speak through it?

  11. 11Technology as constellation

    Interpretation

    No technology exists alone. Every device or platform is a constellation of infrastructures, standards, laws, workers, users, landscapes, histories, and dependencies.

    Catalyst

    What becomes visible when the technological object is examined as a network of relationships rather than as a self-contained thing?

  12. 12Technology as indigestion

    Interpretation

    A technological system may be unable to absorb everything it takes in. Information overload, incompatible values, unresolved histories, toxic waste, and organizational friction return as symptoms of indigestion.

    Catalyst

    What cannot be smoothly incorporated into this system, and how does that unassimilated remainder make itself known?

  13. 13Technology as instillation

    Interpretation

    Technology gradually instils habits, expectations, values, and bodily routines. Through repetition, a designed behavior may come to feel natural, inevitable, or self-chosen.

    Catalyst

    What behavior is being rehearsed until it no longer appears to have been designed?

  14. 14Technology as exaggeration

    Interpretation

    Technology can magnify existing tendencies. A small preference, prejudice, advantage, error, or inequality may become dominant when amplified through automation, replication, or scale.

    Catalyst

    What initially minor tendency becomes disproportionately powerful when this technology extends its reach?

  15. 15Technology as intonation

    Interpretation

    Technology speaks through more than content. Pacing, interface language, notification sounds, animation, friction, and synthetic voices produce an intonation that can communicate urgency, friendliness, authority, or threat.

    Catalyst

    How does the system’s tone shape interpretation and behavior before its actual message has been critically considered?

  16. 16Technology as reincarnation

    Interpretation

    New technologies often give old structures a new body. Surveillance, exploitation, discrimination, colonial extraction, and bureaucratic control may return under the language of personalization, intelligence, efficiency, or innovation.

    Catalyst

    What supposedly obsolete power relationship has been reincarnated under a contemporary technical name?

  17. 17Technology as duration

    Interpretation

    Technology reorganizes time. It accelerates some activities, prolongs others, preserves records, creates waiting, demands maintenance, and produces consequences that may outlast their creators.

    Catalyst

    Who receives immediacy, who is required to wait, and what persists long after its intended moment of use?

  18. 18Technology as cancellation

    Interpretation

    Technology can remove, mute, overwrite, deprecate, automate away, or render obsolete. Cancellation may create space for change, but it may also erase histories, livelihoods, alternatives, and voices.

    Catalyst

    What or whom must disappear for this system to function—and is there any meaningful possibility of appeal or return?

  19. 19Technology as migration

    Interpretation

    Technology moves data, labor, capital, culture, production, and people across territories. It can enable mobility while also creating displacement, dependency, and new borders.

    Catalyst

    What is permitted to move freely through this system, and what—or who—is made immobile?

  20. 20Technology as exploitation

    Interpretation

    Technology may generate value by extracting attention, data, labor, minerals, energy, emotion, or social cooperation. The extraction is often hidden from those who receive the finished service.

    Catalyst

    Whose underpaid, unpaid, involuntary, or environmentally costly contribution makes this technology appear inexpensive and convenient?

  21. 21Technology as implication

    Interpretation

    To use a technology is to become implicated in a wider system of consequences. Participation can connect people to distant labor conditions, environmental damage, surveillance practices, or institutional decisions they did not directly choose.

    Catalyst

    What responsibilities arise from participation, even when the user did not design the system or explicitly consent to all of its effects?

  22. 22Technology as exclamation

    Interpretation

    Technology can punctuate experience with urgency and spectacle. Alerts, launch events, viral metrics, breaking-news banners, and exaggerated promises make certain matters appear immediate while quieting others.

    Catalyst

    What is being made louder, and which slower, subtler, or more ambiguous realities become harder to hear?

  23. 23Technology as investigation

    Interpretation

    Technology extends the capacity to inquire, detect, measure, diagnose, and discover. The same instruments that produce knowledge can also monitor, profile, interrogate, and control.

    Catalyst

    At what point does investigation cease to be a search for understanding and become an apparatus of surveillance?

  24. 24Technology as irritation

    Interpretation

    Friction, annoyance, error, and incompatibility can expose a mismatch between a system and the people expected to use it. Irritation may be a defect, but it may also be a form of resistance or warning.

    Catalyst

    Is the irritation asking for a smoother interface, or is it revealing a deeper problem that should not be designed away?

  25. 25Technology as inhalation

    Interpretation

    Before a system can act, it must take something in: data, resources, cultural material, human attention, biological signals, or environmental inputs. This intake is rarely neutral.

    Catalyst

    What does the technology inhale, from whom is it taken, and under what conditions was it made available?

  26. 26Technology as expiration

    Interpretation

    Technology expires in two senses: it exhales outputs into the world, and it eventually reaches an end. Devices, formats, platforms, data, and skills all acquire afterlives as waste, residue, memory, or abandonment.

    Catalyst

    What does this technology leave behind when it exhales, becomes obsolete, or is declared no longer useful—and who must live with those remains?

  27. 27Technology as digestion

    Interpretation

    Technology processes raw material into usable forms. Data becomes prediction; experience becomes a category; labor becomes an output. Digestion always produces residue, even when the system calls it noise.

    Catalyst

    What is converted into value, what is discarded as waste, and where does that waste accumulate?

  28. 28Technology as action

    Interpretation

    Technology does not simply wait to be used. Defaults, automated decisions, recommendations, constraints, and feedback loops participate in action by shaping what people notice and what they can do.

    Catalyst

    When an outcome occurs, how is agency distributed among users, designers, institutions, algorithms, and infrastructures—and where should responsibility reside?

  29. 29Technology as abstraction

    Interpretation

    Abstraction makes complexity manageable by converting particular people, places, and experiences into categories, numbers, models, or interfaces. What becomes easier to process may become harder to understand in context.

    Catalyst

    Which material, cultural, emotional, or historical details disappear when the situation is translated into an abstract representation?

  30. 30Technology as transaction

    Interpretation

    Technology can reorganize relationships as exchanges. Attention becomes engagement, trust becomes a rating, identity becomes data, care becomes a service, and participation becomes a traceable event.

    Catalyst

    What changes when a relationship is redesigned as a transaction—and what can no longer be given, received, or understood outside the exchange?

  31. 31Technology as contraction

    Interpretation

    Technology can compress time, distance, language, labor, and choice. Such contraction may improve accessibility and efficiency, while also narrowing attention, concentrating power, or reducing complex decisions to limited options.

    Catalyst

    As the process becomes shorter and more efficient, which possibilities, deliberations, or forms of participation contract with it?

  32. 32Technology as distraction

    Interpretation

    Technology directs attention as much as it diverts it. Distraction is not merely an individual failure of discipline; it may be a designed condition through which platforms acquire time, data, and influence.

    Catalyst

    Who benefits when attention is repeatedly interrupted, redirected, or prevented from settling on a difficult question?

  33. 33Technology as dissatisfaction

    Interpretation

    Some technologies depend on maintaining a sense of insufficiency. Continuous updates, comparisons, optimization metrics, and replacement cycles can make satisfaction feel temporary or irresponsible.

    Catalyst

    Is this technology resolving a need, or preserving the dissatisfaction required for continued engagement and consumption?

  34. 34Technology as salivation

    Interpretation

    Interfaces can produce anticipatory desire. Notifications, previews, countdowns, rewards, and personalized cues train users to expect gratification before anything of value has occurred.

    Catalyst

    What cue is conditioning the appetite, and whose interests are served when anticipation becomes habitual?

  35. 35Technology as infatuation

    Interpretation

    Infatuation describes an intense attachment to technical novelty or promise. In this state, possibility is magnified while limitations, dependencies, and harms are interpreted as temporary or irrelevant.

    Catalyst

    What becomes difficult to notice, question, or criticize once people have fallen in love with the proposed solution?

  36. 36Technology as transliteration

    Interpretation

    Transliteration transfers signs from one system into another without guaranteeing that meaning survives. Digitization, automation, and cross-cultural adaptation may preserve form while altering context, nuance, and significance.

    Catalyst

    What remains recognizable after the transfer, and what meaning is lost because it had no equivalent in the receiving system?

  37. 37Technology as reconciliation

    Interpretation

    Technology may help reconcile conflicting records, interests, identities, or needs. Yet a system can also create the appearance of agreement by suppressing unresolved tensions or privileging one version of events.

    Catalyst

    Has the conflict been meaningfully addressed, or has it merely been made less visible and easier to administer?

  38. 38Technology as admiration

    Interpretation

    Technical achievement can inspire legitimate admiration for ingenuity, craft, and collective effort. But admiration may also isolate the celebrated object from the labor, extraction, failures, and alternatives that made it possible.

    Catalyst

    What disappears behind the polished achievement when admiration replaces investigation?

  39. 39Technology as acceleration

    Interpretation

    Technology increases speed, but acceleration is never evenly distributed. Faster production, communication, and decision-making can create pressure, precarity, environmental depletion, and exclusion for those unable or unwilling to keep pace.

    Catalyst

    Faster for whom, toward what destination, and at whose expense?

  40. 40Technology as translocation

    Interpretation

    Technology relocates activity, presence, labor, risk, and harm. A problem may seem to vanish because it has been moved offshore, underground, into a data center, onto another worker, or into another community.

    Catalyst

    Has the problem been solved, or merely transferred beyond the field of vision of those receiving the benefit?

  41. 41Technology as humiliation

    Interpretation

    A technological system can diminish dignity by forcing people to prove their identity, competence, innocence, eligibility, or humanity to an inflexible procedure. Failure becomes public, repetitive, and difficult to contest.

    Catalyst

    Where does the system require people to submit to judgment without being recognized as full participants in that judgment?

  42. 42Technology as multiplication

    Interpretation

    Technology multiplies reach, copies, connections, options, outputs, and consequences. It can distribute access, but it can also reproduce one mistake, prejudice, or decision across immense populations.

    Catalyst

    What happens when this single design decision is repeated thousands or millions of times?

  43. 43Technology as permutation

    Interpretation

    Technology generates novelty by rearranging existing elements. Permutation can reveal unexpected possibilities, yet apparent originality may still reproduce the assumptions embedded in the available materials.

    Catalyst

    Is the result genuinely opening a new possibility, or only rearranging the inherited terms of the old one?

  44. 44Technology as realization

    Interpretation

    Technology gives material form to an idea, desire, or imagined future. Realization also means recognition: once a system exists, it may reveal consequences and conditions that were previously abstract or denied.

    Catalyst

    Which vision has been made real, and which alternative realities became less possible as a result?

  45. 45Technology as rationalization

    Interpretation

    Technology can organize activity according to calculation, standardization, and efficiency. It can also provide an after-the-fact justification, presenting political or moral choices as technically necessary.

    Catalyst

    Which value judgment is being concealed behind the language of optimization, efficiency, objectivity, or inevitability?

  46. 46Technology as variation

    Interpretation

    Technology produces versions, adaptations, configurations, and alternatives. At the same time, those variations may all operate within an underlying standard that determines which differences are permitted.

    Catalyst

    Which forms of variation does the system encourage, and which differences does it correct, exclude, or render unintelligible?

  47. 47Technology as condemnation

    Interpretation

    Technological systems participate in judgment. They mark people, practices, devices, or forms of knowledge as unsafe, fraudulent, obsolete, inefficient, deviant, or unworthy of access.

    Catalyst

    Who or what is being condemned by the system, according to whose standards, and is there a meaningful process of appeal?

  48. 48Technology as augmentation

    Interpretation

    Technology extends perception, memory, mobility, strength, communication, and decision-making. Augmentation can enable participation while also redefining the baseline against which unaugmented people are judged.

    Catalyst

    Whose capacities are enhanced, whose existing capacities are devalued, and what new dependencies accompany the enhancement?

  49. 49Technology as emancipation

    Interpretation

    Technology can expand autonomy, access, expression, solidarity, and freedom from dangerous or oppressive conditions. Yet emancipation remains incomplete when it depends on infrastructures controlled by others.

    Catalyst

    Liberation from what, liberation for whom, and into whose system of ownership, governance, and dependence?

  50. 50Technology as fascination

    Interpretation

    Fascination can sustain attention, curiosity, experimentation, and wonder. It can also hold criticism at a distance, especially when technical complexity or spectacle makes the system seem beyond ordinary judgment.

    Catalyst

    Is fascination drawing the reader into deeper inquiry, or persuading them to suspend judgment?

  51. 51Technology as innovation

    Interpretation

    Innovation names the production of something new, but novelty is not identical to improvement. The term can obscure continuity, maintenance, repair, redistribution, and the recovery of older forms of knowledge.

    Catalyst

    What has genuinely improved, and what has merely been made new, marketable, or difficult to refuse?

  52. 52Technology as speculation

    Interpretation

    Technology is built through imagined futures. Investment, policy, research, and public attention gather around promises about what may become possible, profitable, dangerous, or inevitable.

    Catalyst

    Whose imagined future receives resources, whose future carries the risk, and whose future is treated as expendable?

  53. 53Technology as clarification

    Interpretation

    Technology can make patterns, relationships, and choices more legible. Yet clarification is selective: complexity is reduced, categories are fixed, and ambiguity may be treated as a defect rather than meaningful information.

    Catalyst

    What has become clear only because context, contradiction, or uncertainty was excluded?

  54. 54Technology as lamentation

    Interpretation

    Technology may produce losses that require mourning: lost skills, environments, livelihoods, relationships, memories, futures, or ways of being. Lamentation can turn grief into a form of historical and ethical knowledge.

    Catalyst

    What loss deserves to be named and mourned—and what might that grief teach us about what should be preserved, repaired, or designed differently?

  55. 55Technology as mediation

    Interpretation

    Technology stands between people, objects, institutions, and environments. It does not merely carry an experience or message; it actively shapes the terms on which connection becomes possible.

    Catalyst

    How does the medium change the relationship it appears merely to facilitate?

  56. 56Technology as translation

    Interpretation

    Technology translates between languages, formats, bodies, values, and systems of knowledge. Unlike transliteration, translation attempts to carry meaning—but meaning never crosses unchanged.

    Catalyst

    What becomes distorted, simplified, or untranslatable when experience is transferred into another technical form?

  57. 57Technology as inscription

    Interpretation

    Technologies inscribe assumptions into code, interfaces, standards, architecture, and material objects. A design decision can become a durable rule long after its author has disappeared.

    Catalyst

    Whose values have been written into the system, and who retains the ability to rewrite them?

  58. 58Technology as representation

    Interpretation

    A profile, model, image, metric, or digital twin stands in for something more complex. Representation does not simply depict reality; it determines which version of reality can be recognized and acted upon.

    Catalyst

    Who or what is represented accurately, and who becomes a misleading or incomplete version of themselves?

  59. 59Technology as classification

    Interpretation

    Technology sorts people, objects, behaviors, and events into categories. Classification enables coordination, but it also distributes opportunities, suspicion, visibility, and exclusion.

    Catalyst

    Who defines the categories, and what happens to those who do not fit them?

  60. 60Technology as quantification

    Interpretation

    Quantification translates qualities into numbers: health into scores, popularity into followers, work into productivity, and trust into ratings. What becomes measurable often becomes governable.

    Catalyst

    What important quality is lost when only that which can be counted is treated as real?

  61. 61Technology as standardization

    Interpretation

    Standards make systems interoperable, repeatable, and scalable. They also establish a preferred body, behavior, language, procedure, or worldview against which everything else is treated as deviation.

    Catalyst

    Whose way of living becomes the standard, and whose becomes an exception requiring accommodation?

  62. 62Technology as calibration

    Interpretation

    Every technical system is calibrated to particular thresholds, tolerances, baselines, and definitions of acceptable error. Calibration is therefore not purely technical; it decides which mistakes matter most.

    Catalyst

    To whom is the system calibrated, and who bears the consequences when it is slightly wrong?

  63. 63Technology as prediction

    Interpretation

    Predictive technologies use the past to anticipate future behavior, risk, demand, or possibility. Once institutions act on those forecasts, predictions may help produce the futures they claim merely to foresee.

    Catalyst

    Whose recorded past is being used to limit someone else’s future?

  64. 64Technology as prescription

    Interpretation

    Technology moves from describing what is happening to recommending what should happen. A suggestion, ranking, route, diagnosis, or default can quietly become a command.

    Catalyst

    At what point does technical guidance become a form of governance?

  65. 65Technology as computation

    Interpretation

    Computation makes a situation technically tractable by converting it into formal operations. Yet not every question is computable, and not every computable answer is meaningful or just.

    Catalyst

    What had to be excluded from the problem before the system could calculate a solution?

  66. 66Technology as automation

    Interpretation

    Automation rarely eliminates human participation altogether. It redistributes work toward monitoring, maintenance, correction, training, and exception handling—often making that labor less visible.

    Catalyst

    Whose work disappeared, and whose hidden work became necessary to sustain the appearance of automation?

  67. 67Technology as delegation

    Interpretation

    People delegate perception, memory, judgment, movement, and decision-making to technological systems. Delegation can extend human capacity while making responsibility increasingly difficult to locate.

    Catalyst

    Which decisions may be delegated, and which must remain understandable, contestable, and attributable to a person or institution?

  68. 68Technology as substitution

    Interpretation

    A technical proxy may replace a person, relationship, material, practice, or form of knowledge. The substitute may perform the apparent function while changing the meaning of the activity itself.

    Catalyst

    What essential quality is lost when functional similarity is mistaken for equivalence?

  69. 69Technology as orchestration

    Interpretation

    Technologies coordinate people, machines, data, institutions, and environments into organized performances. Orchestration creates coherence, but it also determines who leads, who follows, and whose contribution remains inaudible.

    Catalyst

    Who sets the score, who determines the tempo, and who is expected to perform without being acknowledged?

  70. 70Technology as synchronization

    Interpretation

    Technology aligns clocks, workflows, markets, bodies, communications, and expectations. Synchronization enables collective activity while imposing a common rhythm on people whose lives may move differently.

    Catalyst

    Who must adjust their time, body, or attention to remain synchronized with the system?

  71. 71Technology as integration

    Interpretation

    Integration joins previously separate tools, databases, institutions, or aspects of life. It can reduce friction, but it can also create concentrated dependency and allow failure or surveillance to travel across the entire system.

    Catalyst

    What autonomy, privacy, or resilience is lost when everything is made to work together?

  72. 72Technology as fragmentation

    Interpretation

    Technology divides activities, identities, labor, and knowledge into discrete units that can be stored, measured, assigned, recombined, and traded. What is easier to manage in fragments may become harder to understand as a whole.

    Catalyst

    What falls between the fragments, and who is responsible for seeing the complete situation?

  73. 73Technology as authorization

    Interpretation

    Technological systems decide who or what is permitted to enter, act, modify, speak, purchase, or know. Authorization turns power into permissions, roles, credentials, and access levels.

    Catalyst

    Who can grant, withdraw, or override permission—and what recourse exists for those who are denied?

  74. 74Technology as regulation

    Interpretation

    Technology regulates behavior through defaults, limits, protocols, architectures, and automated enforcement. Design can govern conduct before a law is invoked or even recognized.

    Catalyst

    Which rules are being enforced through the system, and were those rules ever publicly debated?

  75. 75Technology as administration

    Interpretation

    Technology administers people through records, forms, queues, eligibility checks, schedules, and cases. Administration can coordinate services, but it can also reduce people to procedural objects.

    Catalyst

    When does the management of a person’s case begin to replace attention to the person?

  76. 76Technology as normalization

    Interpretation

    Technology establishes patterns of expected behavior and makes those patterns appear natural. What is repeated, measured, and rewarded becomes normal; what differs becomes suspicious, inefficient, or defective.

    Catalyst

    What kind of person does the system treat as normal, and what happens to those who live otherwise?

  77. 77Technology as securitization

    Interpretation

    Securitization presents a social, political, or technical issue as a threat requiring exceptional monitoring, restriction, or control. The language of safety can make intrusive measures appear unavoidable.

    Catalyst

    What has been defined as a threat, who benefits from that definition, and which freedoms become negotiable once security is invoked?

  78. 78Technology as militarization

    Interpretation

    Military tools and logics can migrate into workplaces, borders, policing, education, entertainment, and ordinary communication. People become targets, environments become operational zones, and uncertainty becomes something to dominate.

    Catalyst

    Which civilian relationship is being reorganized according to the logic of targeting, command, defense, or victory?

  79. 79Technology as colonization

    Interpretation

    Technology can extend control into territories, cultures, languages, bodies, attention, and imagined futures. Colonization occurs when one system extracts from another while replacing local forms of knowledge and authority.

    Catalyst

    What is being occupied or overwritten, and whose definition of development is being imposed?

  80. 80Technology as commodification

    Interpretation

    Commodification turns data, attention, care, intimacy, knowledge, identity, and social cooperation into things that can be priced and exchanged.

    Catalyst

    What becomes ethically or socially diminished when it is redesigned as a marketable asset?

  81. 81Technology as privatization

    Interpretation

    Technologies can transfer infrastructure, knowledge, public space, and decision-making into private ownership. Rights then become services, and public accountability becomes a contractual relationship.

    Catalyst

    What was once collectively governed but can now be accessed only under the owner’s terms?

  82. 82Technology as depoliticization

    Interpretation

    Technology can make a contested political decision appear to be a neutral technical requirement. Questions of justice become questions of efficiency, and disagreements about values become optimization problems.

    Catalyst

    Which political choice is being concealed by the claim that the system simply works this way?

  83. 83Technology as participation

    Interpretation

    Technology can enable people to contribute, communicate, organize, create, and decide together. Yet participation may be symbolic when users can provide input but cannot influence ownership, rules, or outcomes.

    Catalyst

    Are people genuinely sharing power, or merely supplying activity, content, data, and legitimacy?

  84. 84Technology as inclusion

    Interpretation

    Inclusion expands access to systems, services, and public life. But inclusion may require people to adapt themselves to a structure that remains fundamentally unchanged.

    Catalyst

    Is the system accommodating difference, or inviting more people to conform to the same established norm?

  85. 85Technology as exclusion

    Interpretation

    Exclusion may occur through cost, language, connectivity, physical design, identification requirements, skill assumptions, or algorithmic judgment. Those exclusions are often presented as unfortunate side effects rather than design decisions.

    Catalyst

    Who cannot enter, remain, understand, or succeed within the system—and what does their absence allow others not to confront?

  86. 86Technology as negotiation

    Interpretation

    Technology emerges from negotiation among users, designers, regulators, investors, workers, communities, and materials. Even after deployment, people continually negotiate how a system will be interpreted and used.

    Catalyst

    Who is allowed to negotiate the terms of the technology, and who is presented with terms they can only accept or reject?

  87. 87Technology as contestation

    Interpretation

    Technology is a site of struggle over meaning, access, ownership, evidence, and acceptable use. A system is never fully settled when affected people can reinterpret, resist, repair, or challenge it.

    Catalyst

    Can the system’s categories, outputs, and governing assumptions be meaningfully contested?

  88. 88Technology as recognition

    Interpretation

    Technologies recognize faces, voices, gestures, credentials, preferences, and patterns. More broadly, they confer visibility and legitimacy by deciding who or what can be perceived by an institution.

    Catalyst

    What must a person reveal or become in order to be recognized by the system?

  89. 89Technology as misrecognition

    Interpretation

    A system may recognize someone incorrectly, incompletely, or according to a category that distorts their identity. Misrecognition is not only an error; it can become an institutional reality with material consequences.

    Catalyst

    Who carries the burden of proving that the system’s version of them is wrong?

  90. 90Technology as identification

    Interpretation

    Identification binds actions, traces, bodies, and records to a supposedly stable identity. It can enable accountability and access while reducing anonymity, ambiguity, reinvention, and the right not to be known.

    Catalyst

    When is identification necessary, and when should remaining unidentified be protected as a legitimate condition?

  91. 91Technology as personalization

    Interpretation

    Personalization adapts content, services, prices, and environments to an individual. At the same time, it constructs that individual through a profile, often narrowing experience to what the system already expects.

    Catalyst

    Is the technology responding to the person, or producing a more predictable person for the system to manage?

  92. 92Technology as alienation

    Interpretation

    Technology can separate people from their labor, knowledge, communities, environments, decisions, and sense of agency. A process may become more efficient while its participants understand and control less of it.

    Catalyst

    What relationship to one’s work, world, or self becomes more distant as the technology becomes more capable?

  93. 93Technology as production

    Interpretation

    Technology produces more than objects or services. It also produces behaviors, identities, environments, expectations, knowledge, and social orders.

    Catalyst

    Beyond its intended output, what kinds of people, relationships, and worlds does the technology continually produce?

  94. 94Technology as reproduction

    Interpretation

    Technology reproduces cultural patterns, institutional arrangements, inequalities, and forms of knowledge. At scale, it can repeat inherited assumptions while presenting the result as novel or objective.

    Catalyst

    Which existing structure is being reproduced, and what makes that repetition difficult to recognize?

  95. 95Technology as extraction

    Interpretation

    Technology extracts data, minerals, energy, labor, attention, emotion, and knowledge from particular contexts. Extraction separates value from the relationships and environments that generated it.

    Catalyst

    What is being taken, from whom or where, and what—if anything—is returned?

  96. 96Technology as accumulation

    Interpretation

    Technologies allow data, capital, infrastructure, intellectual property, and decision-making power to accumulate over time. Small advantages can compound until participation becomes dependency.

    Catalyst

    What is accumulating, who controls the accumulation, and at what point does advantage become structural power?

  97. 97Technology as depletion

    Interpretation

    Technology consumes finite resources, but depletion is not only environmental. Systems may exhaust attention, patience, skills, community trust, institutional capacity, and the time available for reflection.

    Catalyst

    Which resource is being treated as limitless because its exhaustion is difficult to see or measure?

  98. 98Technology as contamination

    Interpretation

    Technological effects do not remain within their intended boundaries. Chemicals, emissions, misinformation, surveillance practices, insecure code, and behavioral norms can spread into connected environments.

    Catalyst

    What escapes the system, where does it travel, and who must live with consequences they did not consent to?

  99. 99Technology as preservation

    Interpretation

    Technology preserves documents, memories, languages, species, artifacts, and ways of knowing. Yet preservation is selective: something is framed, formatted, frozen, and separated from the conditions in which it once lived.

    Catalyst

    Who decides what deserves to survive, in what form, and what must be omitted to make preservation possible?

  100. 100Technology as restoration

    Interpretation

    Technology promises to restore damaged bodies, ecosystems, archives, infrastructures, and relationships. But restoration never simply returns the past; it constructs a particular version of what the past was and what should be recovered.

    Catalyst

    Whose preferred past is being restored, and which harms or exclusions might return with it?
